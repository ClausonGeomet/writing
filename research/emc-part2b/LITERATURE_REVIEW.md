# Literature review: proposed allocation and distribution objectives

Part 2B tests a **proposed generic heuristic** against frozen Part 2A. It does not reproduce HSC, assert novelty, or use mineral truth during fitting. [Lamberg's HSC Geo manual](https://www.metso.com/globalassets/portfolio/hsc-chemistry/84-geo---mineralogical-calculations-and-mineral-database.pdf) (relevant full-text sections inspected) documents staged mineral rules, chemical residuals and NNLS; its deposit-specific order and sulfide handling cannot be inferred from a uniform minimum-ratio allocator. [Whiten (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1080/08827500701257860) (full publisher PDF inspected) establishes why response rank and feasible sets come before optimizing any order. [Lund et al. (2013)](https://ltu.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2%3A986559) is an institutional abstract for deposit-specific validation, insufficient for algorithm reproduction.

Greedy, beam, random, genetic and exhaustive order search are generic finite-search baselines proposed for this allocation rule. The bounded literature search found no verified primary evidence that a GA or beam method is superior for this exact EMC order problem. This does not establish novelty. A four-phase order space has at most 24 candidates; exhaustive enumeration provides the training-objective oracle. Fixed-`C` constrained LS is a continuous convex problem; integer programming becomes relevant only after a justified discrete selection model. Encoding the allocator's sequence with mixed-integer constraints would add bounds and complexity without an advantage at this scale.

Distribution matching asks whether reconstructed assays resemble observed assays across a population. Under fixed `C`, centered errors and zero abundance-error covariance, the first two moments propagate linearly, but their agreement is necessary at best. Matching each analyte's histogram also leaves cross-analyte dependence free. The exact Anatase/Rutile counterexample in [Part 2A](../emc-part2a/RESEARCH_FINDINGS.md) shows an even stronger limit: distinct mineral populations can yield identical entire assay distributions. The useful opportunity is to test whether auxiliary terms change held-out *mineral* error, then seek independent mineral observations if they do not.
